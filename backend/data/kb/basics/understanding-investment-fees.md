# Understanding Investment Fees

Fees reduce your returns every year, and because they compound, small differences add up to large amounts over decades. Knowing what you pay is one of the few parts of investing fully within your control.

## Common types of fees

- **Expense ratio**: the annual cost of owning a mutual fund or ETF, expressed as a percentage of your investment. It is taken out of the fund's assets automatically, so you never see a bill.
- **Sales loads**: commissions some mutual funds charge when you buy (front-end load) or sell (back-end load).
- **Trading commissions**: fees per trade. Many US brokers now charge no commission for online stock and ETF trades.
- **Advisory fees**: what a financial adviser or robo-adviser charges, often a percentage of assets managed each year.
- **Account fees**: maintenance, inactivity or transfer fees charged by some providers.
- **12b-1 fees**: marketing and distribution fees included in some mutual funds' expense ratios.

## How much fees matter

Suppose two investors each put $100,000 into portfolios that earn the same return before costs for 30 years. One pays 0.10% a year and the other 1.00%. The higher-fee investor can end up with tens of thousands of dollars less, because every year the fee shrinks the base that future growth compounds on.

## Where to find them

A fund's prospectus and fact sheet list its expense ratio and any loads. Brokerage fee schedules list account and trading costs. Investor.gov offers calculators that show the long-term effect of fees.

## Fees are not everything

Low cost does not automatically make an investment right for someone, and a fee may pay for useful services such as planning advice. The point is to know what you pay and what you get for it.
