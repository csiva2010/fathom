# Compound Interest: How Money Grows on Itself

Compound interest means you earn returns not only on the money you put in, but also on the returns that money has already earned. Over long periods this snowball effect becomes the single biggest driver of how large savings can grow.

## Simple versus compound growth

With simple interest, you earn a return only on your original deposit. If you put $1,000 in an account paying 5% simple interest, you earn $50 every year. With compound interest, the first year's $50 is added to your balance, so next year you earn 5% on $1,050, then on a slightly larger amount the year after. The yearly gain keeps growing even though the rate stays the same.

## Why time matters more than timing

Because each year's growth builds on the last, the final years of a long period add far more dollars than the first years. This is why educators stress starting early: someone who begins saving in their twenties gives compounding decades more to work than someone who starts in their forties, even if the later starter saves larger amounts.

## Compounding works against you too

The same math applies to debt. Credit card balances that are not paid in full compound interest on interest, which is why high-rate debt can grow quickly. Fees also compound: a 1% annual fee does not just cost 1% once, it reduces the base that future growth builds on every single year.

## Things to keep in mind

- Investment returns are not guaranteed and vary from year to year; compounding illustrations usually assume a steady average rate for simplicity.
- Frequency matters a little: interest compounded monthly grows slightly faster than interest compounded yearly at the same stated rate.
- Calculators such as the one on Investor.gov let you try different amounts, rates and time periods to see the effect yourself.
