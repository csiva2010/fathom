# How Brokerage Accounts Work

A brokerage account is an account at a broker-dealer that lets you buy and sell investments such as stocks, bonds, ETFs and mutual funds. It is the most common way individuals invest outside of workplace retirement plans.

## Taxable versus retirement accounts

A standard (taxable) brokerage account has no contribution limits and no restrictions on withdrawals, but you owe tax on dividends, interest and realized capital gains each year. Brokers also offer retirement accounts such as IRAs, which have tax advantages but also contribution limits and withdrawal rules.

## Cash and margin accounts

- In a **cash account**, you pay for purchases in full with money in the account.
- In a **margin account**, the broker can lend you money to buy securities, using your investments as collateral. Borrowing magnifies both gains and losses, and the broker can sell your holdings if their value drops too far. Margin is generally considered unsuitable for beginners.

## What happens when you buy

You place an order through the broker, which routes it to an exchange or other trading venue. Once the trade executes, the shares are held in your account, usually in "street name," meaning the broker is the registered holder on your behalf and you are the beneficial owner.

## Protection if a broker fails

The Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) protects customers of member firms if the firm fails and customer assets are missing, up to legal limits. SIPC does not protect against investment losses from market declines.

## Choosing a broker

People commonly compare fees, account minimums, the investment choices available, research tools, ease of use and customer service. FINRA's BrokerCheck lets you look up a firm's registration and disciplinary history.
