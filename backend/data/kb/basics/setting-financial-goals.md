# Setting Financial Goals

Clear goals turn vague intentions like "save more" into concrete plans. They also help decide where money should be kept, because a goal's timeline shapes how much risk makes sense.

## Make goals specific

A useful goal answers four questions: what it is for, how much it will cost, when you need the money, and how much you will set aside each month. "Save $15,000 for a home down payment in four years by putting aside about $310 a month" is far easier to act on than "buy a house someday." Many people use the SMART framework: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

## Group goals by time horizon

- **Short term (under about 3 years)**: emergency fund, a car, a vacation. Usually kept in savings or other stable places, since there is little time to recover from losses.
- **Medium term (about 3 to 10 years)**: a home down payment, education costs. Often a mix of stable assets and some investments.
- **Long term (10 years or more)**: retirement. Usually invested with more growth assets, because there is time to ride out downturns.

## Prioritize

Most people have more goals than money. A common approach is to cover essentials first (an emergency cushion and high-interest debt), take any employer retirement match, and then split remaining savings across goals by importance and deadline.

## Revisit regularly

Income, expenses and priorities change. Checking progress once or twice a year, and after major life events, keeps goals realistic. Projection tools can show whether current contributions are on track and how much would close a gap.
