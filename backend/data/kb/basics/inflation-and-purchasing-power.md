# Inflation and Purchasing Power

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. As prices rise, each dollar buys a little less, which is called a loss of purchasing power. Understanding inflation explains why keeping all long-term savings in cash can quietly lose value.

## How inflation is measured

In the US, the most widely quoted measure is the Consumer Price Index (CPI), published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks the prices of a basket of goods and services that typical households buy, such as food, housing, transportation and medical care. The yearly percentage change in the CPI is the headline inflation rate.

## Nominal versus real returns

A nominal return is the raw percentage your money grew. A real return subtracts inflation. If a savings account pays 3% while inflation runs at 3%, your real return is roughly zero: you have more dollars, but they buy about the same amount as before. Long-term goals are best judged in real terms.

## Why it matters for savers

- Over 20 or 30 years, even moderate inflation can cut purchasing power substantially. At 3% a year, prices roughly double in about 24 years.
- Cash is useful for short-term needs and emergencies, but over long periods it has tended to lag inflation.
- Investments such as stocks have historically outpaced inflation over long periods, though not every year.
- Some bonds, such as Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and Series I savings bonds, are designed to adjust for inflation.

## Inflation and interest rates

The Federal Reserve aims for low, stable inflation over time. When inflation runs high, it may raise short-term interest rates, which affects savings yields, borrowing costs and bond prices.
