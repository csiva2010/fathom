# Initial Public Offerings (IPOs)

An initial public offering, or IPO, is the first time a private company sells shares to the public. Afterward, its shares trade on a stock exchange.

## How an IPO works

The company hires investment banks, called underwriters, to help set the price and sell the shares. It files a registration statement with the SEC, including a prospectus describing the business, finances and risks. The underwriters gauge demand from large investors in a "roadshow," set a final price, and allocate shares, mostly to institutional clients. Trading on the exchange begins the next day.

## Why companies go public

To raise money for growth, to let early investors and employees sell shares, and to gain a public profile. Some companies go public through a direct listing or by merging with a special purpose acquisition company (SPAC) instead.

## What individual investors should know

- **Access**: most IPO shares go to institutions; individuals usually buy once trading starts, often at a different price from the IPO price.
- **Volatility**: new stocks can swing sharply in early trading, with little history to guide expectations.
- **Lock-up periods**: insiders are often barred from selling for a period, commonly around 180 days. When it ends, additional shares may hit the market.
- **Performance**: research has found that IPOs on average have tended to lag the broader market over the following few years, though individual results vary widely.

## Reading the prospectus

The S-1 registration statement, available on the SEC's EDGAR database, includes risk factors, financial statements and how the company plans to use the money raised.
