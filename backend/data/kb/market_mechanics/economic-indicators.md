# Key Economic Indicators

Economic indicators are regularly published statistics that describe the health of the economy. Markets watch them closely because they shape expectations for corporate profits, inflation and interest rates.

## The big ones

- **Gross domestic product (GDP)**: the total value of goods and services produced, reported quarterly by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Two consecutive quarters of decline is a popular, though unofficial, sign of recession.
- **Employment Situation (jobs report)**: published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, with the number of jobs added and the unemployment rate.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: a monthly measure of inflation in consumer prices. "Core" CPI excludes food and energy.
- **PCE price index**: the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure.
- **Retail sales**: monthly consumer spending at stores and online.
- **Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI)**: surveys of business activity; readings above 50 generally indicate expansion.
- **Consumer confidence and sentiment**: surveys of how households feel about the economy.

## Leading, coincident and lagging

Some indicators tend to change before the economy turns (leading, such as building permits and the yield curve), some move with it (coincident, such as employment), and some confirm trends afterward (lagging, such as the unemployment rate).

## Why markets react

Prices move on surprises relative to forecasts. A strong jobs report can lift stocks by signaling growth, or push them down if investors fear it will keep the Fed raising rates. The same data can be read different ways depending on what the market is most worried about at the time.
