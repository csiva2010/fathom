# The VIX and Market Volatility

The Cboe Volatility Index, known as the VIX, measures how much volatility investors expect in the S&P 500 over the next 30 days. It is often called the market's "fear gauge."

## What it measures

The VIX is calculated from the prices of S&P 500 index options. When investors expect bigger price swings, they pay more for options as protection, which raises the VIX. It is expressed as an annualized percentage: a VIX of 20 implies expected annualized volatility of about 20%, which works out to typical daily moves of a bit over 1%.

## Typical levels

- Below about 15: relatively calm, confident markets.
- Around 15 to 25: normal to moderately uneasy.
- Above about 30: high fear, often during sell-offs.

The VIX spiked above 80 in the 2008 financial crisis and in March 2020.

## How it relates to stocks

The VIX usually rises when stocks fall sharply and drifts lower in steady bull markets. It is forward-looking, based on expectations, unlike historical volatility, which measures what already happened.

## Can you invest in it?

You cannot buy the VIX itself. Products that track VIX futures exist, but they tend to lose value over time in calm markets because of how futures prices roll. Regulators warn they are complex, short-term trading tools rather than long-term investments.

## A long-term perspective

High VIX readings reflect fear in the moment. Historically, periods of extreme fear have sometimes been followed by recoveries, but timing those is notoriously difficult.
