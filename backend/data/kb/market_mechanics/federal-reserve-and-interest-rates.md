# The Federal Reserve and Interest Rates

The Federal Reserve, or the Fed, is the central bank of the United States. Its decisions about interest rates ripple through savings accounts, loans, bond prices and stock markets.

## The dual mandate

Congress gave the Fed two main goals: maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed interprets price stability as inflation of 2% over time, measured by the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index.

## The federal funds rate

The Fed's main tool is a target range for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets eight times a year to set it.

- **Raising rates** makes borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow spending and inflation.
- **Cutting rates** makes borrowing cheaper, which tends to support spending and employment.

## How rate changes reach you

- Savings account, CD and money market yields generally move with the Fed's rate.
- Credit card and other variable-rate loans adjust quickly.
- Mortgage rates are more closely tied to longer-term Treasury yields, which reflect expectations about future Fed policy and inflation.
- Bond prices fall when rates rise, especially long-term bonds.
- Stock valuations can be affected, since higher rates make future earnings worth less today and raise companies' borrowing costs.

## Other tools

The Fed also buys or sells large amounts of Treasury and mortgage-backed securities (quantitative easing or tightening) and communicates its outlook to guide expectations.

## Markets react to expectations

Markets often move more on surprises and on changes in the Fed's projected path than on the announced decision itself, because expected moves are usually already priced in.
