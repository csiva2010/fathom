# Short Selling

Short selling is a way to profit if a stock's price falls. It is the reverse of the usual buy-low, sell-high sequence: sell high first, buy low later.

## How it works

1. The short seller borrows shares, usually through a broker, and sells them at today's price.
2. Later, they buy the shares back, hopefully at a lower price, and return them to the lender.
3. The profit is the difference, minus borrowing fees and costs.

Short selling requires a margin account.

## The risks

- **Unlimited loss potential**: a stock can only fall to zero, limiting a buyer's loss, but it can rise without limit, so a short seller's possible loss has no ceiling.
- **Margin calls**: if the price rises, the broker can demand more collateral or close the position at a loss.
- **Borrowing costs**: hard-to-borrow stocks can carry high fees.
- **Dividends**: short sellers must pay any dividends to the lender.
- **Short squeezes**: when a heavily shorted stock rises, short sellers rushing to buy back shares can drive prices even higher, as seen in the meme-stock surge of early 2021.

## Why it exists

Short sellers can help markets by expressing negative views, improving price discovery and sometimes uncovering fraud. Institutions also use shorting to hedge other positions.

## Short interest

Short interest, the number of shares sold short, is published regularly. High short interest shows that many investors expect a decline, and it also means more potential buying pressure if they are forced to cover.
