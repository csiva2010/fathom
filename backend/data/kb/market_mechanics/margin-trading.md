# Buying on Margin

Buying on margin means borrowing money from your broker to buy securities, using the securities in your account as collateral. It is a form of leverage: it magnifies both gains and losses.

## How it works

Under Federal Reserve rules, you can generally borrow up to 50% of the purchase price of eligible stocks. With $10,000 of your own money, you could buy up to $20,000 of stock. You pay interest on the borrowed amount, often at rates well above mortgage or savings rates.

## Leverage cuts both ways

If the $20,000 position rises 20% to $24,000, your equity grows from $10,000 to $14,000, a 40% gain before interest. If it falls 20% to $16,000, your equity drops to $6,000, a 40% loss. A large enough fall can wipe out more than you invested.

## Margin calls

Brokers require you to keep a minimum level of equity, the maintenance requirement, set by FINRA rules at no less than 25% and often higher at individual firms. If your equity falls below it, you get a margin call and must add cash or securities. The broker can also sell your holdings without notice, at the worst possible time, to cover the shortfall, and you are responsible for any remaining debt.

## Who uses it

Margin is used by active traders and some sophisticated investors. Regulators caution that it is risky and generally not appropriate for beginners. Many brokers enable margin by default on certain account types, so it is worth knowing whether your account has it.
