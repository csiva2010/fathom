# What Market Indexes Are

A market index is a measurement of how a group of securities is performing. It works like a scoreboard for part of the market. You cannot invest in an index directly, but many funds are built to track one.

## The best-known US indexes

The S&P 500 tracks about 500 large US companies and is the most common yardstick for the US stock market. It is weighted by market capitalization, so the largest companies have the biggest influence. The Nasdaq Composite includes several thousand stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange and leans heavily toward technology. The Dow Jones Industrial Average follows 30 large companies and is price-weighted, which means higher-priced shares move it more regardless of company size.

## How weighting changes the story

Because of market-cap weighting, a handful of very large companies can drive much of the S&P 500's daily move. An equal-weight version of the same index can tell a different story on the same day. Knowing how an index is built helps you interpret headlines like "the market fell 1%."

## Indexes as benchmarks

Investors and fund managers compare their results to an index that matches what they invest in. A US large-company stock fund is usually compared to the S&P 500; a bond fund might be compared to a broad bond index. Comparing a portfolio to the right benchmark shows whether its risk and return are reasonable for what it holds.

## Index funds and ETF proxies

Index funds and ETFs try to match an index by owning its members in the same proportions. Some ETFs are so closely tied to an index that people use them as stand-ins: SPY for the S&P 500, QQQ for the Nasdaq-100 and DIA for the Dow. Their prices differ from the index level, but their percentage moves are very similar.

## Reading index moves

Daily moves are usually reported as points and percentages. Percentages are more meaningful because a 300-point move means very different things for indexes at different levels.
