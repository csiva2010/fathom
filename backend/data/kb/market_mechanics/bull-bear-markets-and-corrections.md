# Bull Markets, Bear Markets and Corrections

Markets move in long swings. A few common terms describe the size of those moves.

## Definitions

- **Bull market**: a sustained rise in prices, often defined as a 20% gain from a recent low.
- **Correction**: a decline of 10% to 20% from a recent high.
- **Bear market**: a decline of 20% or more from a recent high.

These thresholds are conventions, not official rules, and are usually applied to broad indexes such as the S&P 500.

## How often they happen

Corrections have been fairly common historically, occurring roughly every year or two on average, though with no regular schedule. Bear markets have been less frequent, often associated with recessions or financial crises. US bull markets have, on average, lasted much longer than bear markets and produced gains that more than offset prior losses, though there is no guarantee that pattern continues.

## Recent examples

- The 2007 to 2009 financial crisis bear market, in which US stocks fell more than 50%.
- The rapid 2020 bear market at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by an unusually fast recovery.
- The 2022 bear market, when rising interest rates pushed both stocks and bonds down.

## What it means for investors

No one has a reliable track record of predicting when bear markets will start or end. Long-term investors often plan for them in advance by holding an allocation they can stick with, keeping an emergency fund so they are not forced to sell, and continuing regular contributions during downturns.
