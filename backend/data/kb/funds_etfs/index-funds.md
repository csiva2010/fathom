# Index Funds

An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF that aims to match the performance of a market index, such as the S&P 500 or the total US stock market, rather than trying to beat it.

## How they work

Instead of a manager picking stocks, an index fund holds all (or a representative sample) of the securities in its index, in the same proportions. When the index changes its members, the fund follows. This approach is called passive investing.

## Why many investors use them

- **Low cost**: with no research team picking securities, expense ratios are often a small fraction of a percent.
- **Broad diversification**: a single total market fund can hold thousands of companies.
- **Tax efficiency**: low turnover means fewer taxable capital gains distributions, especially in ETF form.
- **Simplicity and transparency**: you know what you own and what to expect relative to the market.

Research has consistently found that most actively managed funds trail comparable index funds after fees over long periods, which is a big part of index funds' appeal.

## What to know

An index fund will fall when its market falls; it does not try to avoid downturns. It also inherits its index's quirks: market-cap-weighted indexes put more money in the largest companies. And not every product called an "index fund" is broad or cheap; niche indexes can be narrow and costly.

## Choosing among them

Common points of comparison are the index tracked, the expense ratio, how closely the fund has matched its index (tracking difference), and whether a mutual fund or ETF structure suits your account.
