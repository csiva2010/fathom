# ETFs vs Mutual Funds

Both exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and mutual funds pool money from many investors to buy a basket of securities. One share of a fund gives you a small slice of everything the fund owns, which makes diversification easy even with a small amount of money.

## How they trade

ETF shares trade on an exchange throughout the day, like a stock, and the price changes minute by minute. Mutual fund shares are bought and sold directly with the fund company once per day, at a price called net asset value (NAV) calculated after the market closes.

## Costs

Every fund charges an annual fee called the expense ratio, expressed as a percentage of your investment. A 0.03% expense ratio costs $3 a year on $10,000, while a 1% expense ratio costs $100. Over decades, this difference compounds and can noticeably reduce what you end up with. Some mutual funds also charge sales loads or have minimum investments; most ETFs do not have minimums beyond the price of one share, and many brokers allow fractional shares.

## Index funds and active funds

An index fund tries to match the return of a market index, such as the S&P 500, by holding the same securities. Because it does not need a team picking investments, it usually has low costs. An actively managed fund tries to beat the market by selecting investments, which usually costs more. Research has repeatedly found that most active funds trail their benchmark index over long periods after fees.

## Taxes

In taxable accounts, ETFs are often more tax-efficient than mutual funds because of how shares are created and redeemed, which tends to reduce capital-gains distributions. Inside retirement accounts like a 401(k) or IRA, this difference does not matter.

## Choosing between them

For many investors the choice of fund matters less than its cost, what it holds, and whether it fits their plan. Both structures can hold the same index.
