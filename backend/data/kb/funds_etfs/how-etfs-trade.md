# How ETFs Trade: Spreads, Premiums and Liquidity

ETFs trade on stock exchanges throughout the day, like individual stocks. That flexibility comes with a few mechanics worth understanding.

## Market price versus NAV

An ETF has a net asset value (NAV), the per-share value of its holdings, and a market price set by trading. Usually the two stay very close. When the market price is above NAV the ETF trades at a premium; below, at a discount.

## The creation and redemption process

Large financial firms called authorized participants can create new ETF shares by delivering the underlying securities to the fund, or redeem shares for those securities. If an ETF's price drifts above its NAV, they can profit by creating shares and selling them, which pushes the price back down, and vice versa. This arbitrage keeps prices in line and is also why ETFs tend to be tax efficient.

## The bid-ask spread

Every trade has a spread between the highest bid and lowest ask. For heavily traded ETFs, the spread can be a cent or two. For thinly traded or niche ETFs, it can be much wider, adding a hidden cost each time you buy or sell. Using limit orders helps control the price you pay.

## Liquidity comes from the holdings

An ETF's liquidity depends mostly on how easy its underlying securities are to trade, not only on its own trading volume. A low-volume ETF holding large US stocks can still trade efficiently.

## Timing tips commonly mentioned

Spreads can be wider in the first and last minutes of the trading day and during market turmoil, and international ETFs can drift from NAV when foreign markets are closed.
