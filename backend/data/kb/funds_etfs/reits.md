# Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)

A real estate investment trust, or REIT, is a company that owns, operates or finances income-producing real estate, such as apartments, offices, warehouses, data centers or shopping centers. REITs let investors own a slice of real estate without buying property directly.

## How REITs work

To qualify as a REIT under US tax law, a company must, among other rules, pay out at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders as dividends. In return, it generally does not pay corporate income tax on that income. This is why REITs typically have higher dividend yields than most stocks.

## Types

- **Equity REITs** own and manage properties and earn rent.
- **Mortgage REITs** lend money or buy mortgages and earn interest; they can be especially sensitive to interest rates.
- **Publicly traded REITs** trade on exchanges. Non-traded REITs do not, and can be hard to sell and expensive; regulators have warned investors about their risks.

## Risks

REITs are stocks, so their prices can be volatile, and they are sensitive to interest rates and to conditions in their property sectors, as offices showed after remote work spread. They tend to move with the broader stock market, offering less diversification than some expect.

## Taxes

Most REIT dividends are not "qualified" and are taxed as ordinary income in taxable accounts, although some may qualify for a deduction. Many investors hold REITs in tax-advantaged accounts.

## Fund exposure

Broad stock index funds already include publicly traded REITs. Real estate sector funds add more concentrated exposure.
