# Anchoring Bias

Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first number or piece of information we see, and to adjust insufficiently from it. In investing, the anchor is often a price.

## Common anchors

- **Your purchase price**: "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid." The market does not know or care what you paid. The real question is whether you would buy the investment today at today's price.
- **A past high**: a stock that once traded at $200 and now trades at $100 is not necessarily cheap; the business may be worth much less than before.
- **Round numbers and targets**: fixating on a specific portfolio value or index level.
- **The first estimate you hear**: an analyst's price target or a headline forecast can frame later judgments.

## Why it matters

Anchoring can keep investors in losing positions too long, make them miss good opportunities that look expensive compared with an old price, and lead to poorly reasoned buy and sell targets. It also affects big life decisions, such as home prices and salary negotiations.

## Ways to reduce it

- Evaluate each holding as if you were deciding to buy it today.
- Base decisions on goals, diversification and fundamentals rather than past prices.
- Use percentages and ranges rather than single precise targets.
- Consider several reference points, not just one.

Anchoring happens automatically, so building rules into your process helps more than simply trying to ignore past prices.
