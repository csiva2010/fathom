# Common Investor Biases

Behavioral finance studies how real people make money decisions, which is often less rational than textbook models assume. Knowing the common mental shortcuts can help you notice them in your own choices.

## Loss aversion

Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equal-sized gains feel good. This can lead investors to sell in a panic during a downturn, locking in losses, or to hold on to a losing investment too long hoping to "get back to even."

## Herd behavior and fear of missing out

When everyone seems to be buying something, it is tempting to join in, often after prices have already risen a lot. The same instinct can drive mass selling in a crash. Following the crowd tends to mean buying high and selling low.

## Overconfidence

Many investors believe they can pick winning stocks or time the market better than average. Overconfidence often shows up as frequent trading, which raises costs and taxes and, according to many studies, tends to lower returns.

## Recency bias

People give too much weight to what happened recently. After a strong year, risk feels low; after a sharp drop, it feels like the decline will never end. Long-term history provides a steadier perspective than the last few months.

## Anchoring

Anchoring means fixing on a particular number, such as the price you paid for a stock, and judging every later decision against it. The market does not know or care what you paid; the useful question is whether the investment still fits your plan today.

## Confirmation bias

We tend to seek out information that agrees with what we already believe and ignore what does not. Deliberately reading the opposite view is a simple counterweight.

## Practical defenses

Writing down a plan before markets move, automating contributions, rebalancing on a schedule, and waiting a day before acting on strong emotions are common ways investors reduce the influence of these biases.
