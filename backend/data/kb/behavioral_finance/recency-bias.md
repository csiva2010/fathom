# Recency Bias

Recency bias is the tendency to give too much weight to recent events and assume they will continue. In investing, it often leads people to chase what has just done well and abandon what has just done poorly.

## Examples

- After a few strong years for stocks, investors may take more risk than they can handle, assuming gains will continue.
- After a crash, they may avoid stocks for years, assuming losses will continue, and miss the recovery.
- Buying the fund at the top of last year's performance list, even though top performers rarely stay on top.
- Assuming the asset class or country that led over the last decade will lead the next.

## Why it is costly

Markets move in cycles, and leadership rotates. US and international stocks, growth and value, and large and small companies have each taken turns outperforming. Chasing recent winners tends to mean buying after prices have risen and selling after they have fallen, the opposite of what investors intend.

## Ways to counter it

- Look at long histories, multiple decades, not just the last few years.
- Diversify across asset classes so that you always own some of what is currently doing well and some of what is lagging.
- Rebalance on a schedule, which systematically trims recent winners and adds to recent laggards.
- Judge investments on costs, diversification and fit with your plan rather than recent returns.

Fund documents carry the warning that past performance does not guarantee future results for a reason: recent returns are a poor predictor of future returns.
