# Market Timing vs Time in the Market

Market timing means trying to move in and out of investments based on predictions of where prices are headed: selling before a fall and buying before a rise. It is appealing in theory, and very hard in practice.

## Why it is so difficult

To succeed, you must be right twice: when to get out and when to get back in. Markets often rebound sharply and unexpectedly, and many of the best days in market history have come shortly after the worst days, during periods of high fear. Investors who sell during a downturn often miss those rebounds.

## The cost of missing the best days

Studies of the S&P 500 over long periods show that missing just a handful of the best-performing days would have greatly reduced total returns compared with staying invested. Because those days are unpredictable and cluster around turmoil, sitting out to avoid losses frequently means missing gains too.

## Professionals struggle too

Research on tactical and market-timing funds has generally found that they do not reliably beat simple buy-and-hold strategies after costs.

## Alternatives

- **Time in the market**: invest according to a long-term plan and stay invested through ups and downs.
- **Dollar-cost averaging**: invest fixed amounts on a schedule, removing the decision of when.
- **Rebalancing**: adjusting back to target weights is a rules-based form of "buying low and selling high" without predictions.

## When adjusting makes sense

Changing an allocation because your goals, time horizon or risk capacity changed is different from reacting to market forecasts. The first is planning; the second is timing.
