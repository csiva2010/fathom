# Mental Accounting

Mental accounting, a concept developed by economist Richard Thaler, describes how people sort money into separate mental categories and treat it differently depending on its source or intended use, even though a dollar is a dollar.

## Examples

- Spending a tax refund or bonus freely as "found money," while carefully guarding regular salary.
- Keeping cash in a low-interest savings account while carrying a high-interest credit card balance.
- Treating gains in a stock as "house money" and taking bigger risks with it.
- Refusing to sell a losing investment because closing that mental account would make the loss real.
- Evaluating each holding separately instead of looking at the whole portfolio.

## When it helps

Mental accounting is not always harmful. Labeled savings buckets, such as an emergency fund, a sinking fund for car repairs and a down payment fund, can make saving easier and protect money from being spent on impulse. Many budgeting methods use this tendency deliberately.

## When it hurts

It becomes costly when it leads to paying more interest than necessary, taking inconsistent risks, or judging investments in isolation. A portfolio should be evaluated as a whole: a holding that zigs while others zag can be valuable even if it looks like a loser on its own.

## A useful check

Ask whether a decision would change if the money had come from a different source. If yes, mental accounting may be at work.
