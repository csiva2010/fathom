# Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency to feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equal gain. Research by psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky suggested losses can feel roughly twice as intense as gains of the same size.

## How it shows up in investing

- **Panic selling**: a market drop feels so painful that investors sell to make the feeling stop, often near the bottom, locking in losses and missing the recovery.
- **Holding losers too long**: selling at a loss makes it "real," so people hang on, hoping to get back to even. This is called the disposition effect.
- **Selling winners too early**: locking in a gain feels good, so people cut their best investments short.
- **Too much caution**: avoiding stocks entirely because of fear of losses, and missing long-term growth.

## Why it matters

Investor return studies have repeatedly found that the average investor's returns trail the returns of the funds they own, largely because of poorly timed buying and selling. Emotional reactions to losses are a major cause.

## Ways people counter it

- Choose an asset allocation in calm times that you can live with in bad times.
- Automate contributions and rebalancing so decisions do not depend on mood.
- Look at your portfolio less often during turbulent periods.
- Focus on long-term goals rather than daily price changes.
- Write down an investment policy statement explaining what you will do in a downturn.

Understanding that loss aversion is a normal human reaction makes it easier to recognize the feeling without acting on it.
