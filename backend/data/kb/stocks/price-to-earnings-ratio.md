# The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, compares a company's share price with its earnings per share. It is one of the most widely quoted ways to describe how expensive a stock is relative to the profits behind it.

## How it is calculated

P/E = share price / earnings per share (EPS). If a stock trades at $50 and the company earned $2.50 per share over the past year, the P/E is 20. Put another way, investors are paying $20 for each $1 of annual earnings.

- **Trailing P/E** uses the last 12 months of reported earnings.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' estimates of the next 12 months, which may turn out wrong.

## How people interpret it

A higher P/E usually means investors expect faster future earnings growth, or see the company as lower risk. A lower P/E can mean slower expected growth, higher risk, or a stock the market has overlooked. P/E means little in isolation; it is most useful when compared with the same company's history, its industry peers, or the broader market.

## Limitations

- Companies with losses have no meaningful P/E.
- Earnings can be distorted by one-time items, accounting choices or a cyclical peak or trough.
- Different industries normally trade at very different P/Es; a software firm and a utility are not comparable on P/E alone.
- A low P/E is not automatically a bargain, and a high one is not automatically overpriced.

## Related measures

Analysts also use price-to-sales, price-to-book, the PEG ratio (P/E divided by expected growth) and dividend yield. Each captures a different angle on valuation, and none predicts short-term price moves.
