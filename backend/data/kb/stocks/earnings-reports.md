# Understanding Earnings Reports

Public companies report their financial results every quarter. Earnings season, when most companies report, is often a time of bigger stock price moves.

## Where the numbers come from

US public companies file quarterly reports (Form 10-Q) and an annual report (Form 10-K) with the SEC, available free on the SEC's EDGAR database. Companies usually also publish a press release and hold a conference call with analysts.

## Key figures

- **Revenue**: total sales for the period.
- **Net income**: profit after all expenses and taxes.
- **Earnings per share (EPS)**: net income divided by shares outstanding. "Diluted" EPS accounts for stock options and convertible securities.
- **Margins**: profit as a percentage of revenue, showing how efficiently the company operates.
- **Guidance**: management's outlook for upcoming quarters.

## Expectations drive the reaction

Prices often move based on results versus expectations rather than on the results alone. Analysts publish estimates, and a company that "beats" estimates may still fall if its guidance disappoints, while one that "misses" may rise if the outlook improves. This is why a strong report sometimes leads to a falling stock.

## Adjusted versus GAAP results

Companies must report under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), but many also highlight "adjusted" figures that exclude items such as restructuring costs. Adjusted numbers can be useful but may flatter results, so comparing both is worthwhile.

## A long-term perspective

One quarter rarely defines a business. Trends across several years of revenue, profit and cash flow say more than any single report.
