# Common vs Preferred Stock

Companies can issue two main kinds of stock. Most individual investors own common stock, but preferred stock has features of both stocks and bonds.

## Common stock

Common shareholders own part of the company and usually get to vote on matters such as electing the board of directors. They share in the company's growth through rising share prices and any dividends. In a bankruptcy, common shareholders are paid last, after creditors and preferred shareholders, and often receive nothing.

## Preferred stock

Preferred stock typically pays a fixed dividend and gets priority over common stock for dividends and in a liquidation. In exchange, preferred shareholders usually have no voting rights and limited upside: the price tends to move with interest rates rather than with the company's growth, much like a bond.

Common features include:

- **Cumulative dividends**: missed dividends must be paid before common shareholders get any.
- **Callable**: the issuer can buy the shares back at a set price after a certain date.
- **Convertible**: some preferred shares can be exchanged for common stock.

## Risk comparison

Preferred stock is generally less volatile than common stock but is not as safe as high-quality bonds. Dividends can be suspended, and many preferreds are issued by banks and other financial companies, which concentrates risk. Rising interest rates tend to push preferred prices down.
