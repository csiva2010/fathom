# Stock Splits and Share Buybacks

Stock splits and share buybacks both change a company's share count, but they work very differently.

## Stock splits

In a stock split, a company divides each existing share into several. In a 2-for-1 split, a shareholder with 100 shares at $200 ends up with 200 shares at about $100. The total value of the holding and the company's market cap do not change; the pie is simply cut into more slices.

Companies split shares to make the price per share lower, which some believe makes the stock more accessible. With fractional-share trading now common at many brokers, a high share price matters less than it once did.

A **reverse split** combines shares, for example 1-for-10, raising the price per share. Companies sometimes do this to meet an exchange's minimum price requirement, which can signal financial trouble.

## Share buybacks

In a buyback, the company uses cash to repurchase its own shares on the market. Fewer shares outstanding means each remaining share represents a larger slice of the company, and earnings per share rise even if total earnings do not.

Supporters see buybacks as a tax-efficient way to return cash to shareholders. Critics argue they can be poorly timed, boost per-share metrics tied to executive pay, or use cash that could fund investment. Buybacks, unlike dividends, do not create a taxable event for shareholders who do not sell.

## Key takeaway

A split changes the number and price of shares without changing value. A buyback actually changes ownership proportions and uses company cash.
