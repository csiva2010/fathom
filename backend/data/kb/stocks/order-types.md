# Market, Limit and Stop Orders

When you buy or sell a stock or ETF, the order type tells your broker how to execute the trade. The choice affects the price you get and whether the trade happens at all.

## Market order

A market order buys or sells right away at the best available price. It almost always executes, but the price is not guaranteed. In fast-moving markets, or for thinly traded securities, the execution price can differ noticeably from the last quote you saw.

## Limit order

A limit order sets the worst price you will accept: a maximum for a buy or a minimum for a sell. It protects you from an unexpectedly bad price, but it may never execute if the market does not reach your limit. Limit orders are often recommended for less liquid securities and for trading outside regular hours.

## Stop order

A stop order (stop-loss) becomes a market order once the price reaches a trigger level, the stop price. It is often used to limit losses. Because it turns into a market order, the actual sale price can be well below the stop price if the stock gaps down, for example overnight.

A **stop-limit order** becomes a limit order at the trigger instead, which controls price but may not execute at all in a sharp fall.

## Time in force

Orders also specify how long they stay active: a **day order** expires at the end of the trading day, while a **good-til-canceled** order stays open until filled or canceled, subject to the broker's maximum.
