# Capital Gains Tax Basics

When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain. In a taxable account, that gain is generally subject to tax in the year you sell.

## Realized versus unrealized

A gain is unrealized while you still own the investment; it becomes realized when you sell. You generally owe tax only on realized gains (dividends and interest are taxed separately when paid).

## Short-term versus long-term

- **Short-term gains**, on investments held one year or less, are taxed at your ordinary income tax rate.
- **Long-term gains**, on investments held more than one year, are taxed at lower rates: 0%, 15% or 20% depending on taxable income. Some higher-income taxpayers also owe a 3.8% net investment income tax.

Holding just past the one-year mark can noticeably reduce the tax on a gain.

## Cost basis

Your gain is the sale price minus your cost basis, which is generally what you paid including fees, adjusted for things like reinvested dividends. Brokers report basis for most securities bought in recent years on Form 1099-B. If you bought shares at different times, the lots you sell (for example first-in-first-out or specific identification) affect the gain.

## Capital losses

Losses offset gains. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 a year can offset ordinary income, and the rest carries forward to future years. The wash sale rule limits claiming a loss if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days.

## Retirement accounts are different

Buying and selling inside IRAs and 401(k)s does not trigger capital gains tax. Traditional accounts are taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn, and qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free.
