# The Backdoor Roth IRA

High earners cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA once their income passes the IRS limit. The "backdoor Roth" is a two-step process that lets them end up with Roth money anyway, using rules that permit it.

## The two steps

1. **Make a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA.** Anyone with earned income can contribute to a traditional IRA, but high earners covered by a workplace plan get no deduction. They report the contribution on Form 8606, creating after-tax basis.
2. **Convert the traditional IRA to a Roth IRA.** There is no income limit on conversions. If the money is converted quickly, there is little or no growth to tax.

## The pro-rata rule

This is the main trap. If you have other pre-tax money in any traditional, SEP or SIMPLE IRA, the IRS treats a conversion as coming proportionally from pre-tax and after-tax money across all of those IRAs. For example, with $93,000 pre-tax in an old rollover IRA plus a $7,000 nondeductible contribution, 93% of the converted amount would be taxable. Some people roll pre-tax IRA money into a workplace 401(k), if allowed, to clear the way. The calculation uses IRA balances as of December 31 of the conversion year.

## Other points

- Each conversion has its own five-year clock for penalty-free withdrawal of converted amounts before age 59½.
- Form 8606 must be filed correctly; mistakes are common.
- A related strategy, the "mega backdoor Roth," uses after-tax 401(k) contributions, if a plan allows them.

Because of the tax-reporting details, many people review a backdoor Roth with a tax professional the first time.
