# Early Withdrawals from Retirement Accounts

Retirement accounts get tax breaks on the condition that the money is used for retirement. Taking money out before age 59½ usually triggers a 10% additional tax on top of any regular income tax owed.

## How the cost adds up

For a traditional IRA or 401(k), an early withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income plus the 10% additional tax. Someone in the 22% bracket who withdraws $10,000 early could owe about $3,200 in federal tax alone, plus any state tax, and loses the future tax-advantaged growth on that money.

## Common exceptions to the 10% tax

The IRS lists many exceptions, and some apply only to IRAs or only to workplace plans. Examples include:

- Leaving your employer in or after the year you turn 55 (for that employer's plan).
- Total and permanent disability.
- Unreimbursed medical expenses above a percentage of income.
- A series of substantially equal periodic payments (often called 72(t) payments).
- First-time home purchase, up to a lifetime limit (IRAs only).
- Qualified higher education expenses (IRAs only).
- Birth or adoption expenses, up to a limit.
- Certain emergency personal expenses, up to a limit, under SECURE 2.0.

Income tax usually still applies even when the 10% tax does not.

## Roth IRAs are different

You can withdraw your own Roth IRA contributions at any time without tax or penalty. Earnings are tax-free only for qualified distributions, generally after age 59½ and five years.

## Alternatives people consider first

Many plans allow 401(k) loans, and hardship withdrawals have their own rules. Because early withdrawals are costly, an emergency fund outside retirement accounts is often the first line of defense.
