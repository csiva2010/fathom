# Maximum Drawdown

Maximum drawdown is the largest percentage drop from a peak to a later low over a given period. It answers a very practical question: "how bad has it gotten?"

## How it is calculated

Track the investment's value over time. At each point, compare it with the highest value reached so far. The biggest gap, measured as a percentage of the peak, is the maximum drawdown. If a fund rose to $120, fell to $78, then recovered, its drawdown is (120 - 78) / 120 = 35%.

## Some historical context

Broad US stock indexes have experienced several severe drawdowns, including roughly 50% in 2000 to 2002, more than 50% in 2007 to 2009, and about 34% in early 2020, which recovered unusually quickly. Balanced stock and bond portfolios typically had smaller drawdowns.

## Recovery takes more than the fall

A loss requires a larger percentage gain to get back to even: a 20% drop needs a 25% gain, and a 50% drop needs a 100% gain. Recovery times after major crashes have ranged from months to more than a decade.

## Why it matters

Drawdown captures the experience of living through a downturn better than volatility alone. Many people's real limit is not volatility but the largest loss they can watch without abandoning their plan. Looking at past drawdowns of an allocation is one way to test whether its risk is acceptable.

## Limits

A past maximum drawdown is not a worst case for the future. Short histories can understate risk. This assistant shows the max drawdown over the data window it uses, labeled with that window.
