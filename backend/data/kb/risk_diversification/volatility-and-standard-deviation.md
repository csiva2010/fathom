# Volatility and Standard Deviation

Volatility describes how much an investment's price swings up and down. It is the most common way to measure investment risk, usually expressed as the standard deviation of returns.

## Standard deviation in plain terms

Standard deviation measures how spread out returns are around their average. A fund with an average return of 8% and a standard deviation of 15% has had most yearly returns fall roughly between -7% and +23% (one standard deviation either side), and some years outside that range.

- Low standard deviation: steadier returns, like short-term bonds.
- High standard deviation: wider swings, like single stocks or emerging markets.

## Annualized volatility

Volatility is often calculated from daily returns and then annualized so different investments can be compared on the same yearly scale. This assistant's market data shows 30-day annualized volatility for each ticker, which captures how jumpy the price has been recently.

## What volatility does and does not tell you

Volatility captures how bumpy the ride is, which matters for investors who might need to sell during a downturn or who might panic. It does not capture every risk: a bond can show low volatility right up until it defaults, and volatility treats big gains the same as big losses.

## Related measures

- **Beta**: volatility relative to the overall market.
- **Maximum drawdown**: the largest peak-to-trough fall over a period.
- **Sharpe ratio**: return earned per unit of volatility above a risk-free rate.

Combining several measures gives a fuller picture than any one number.
