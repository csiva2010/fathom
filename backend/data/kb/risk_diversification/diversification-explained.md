# Diversification Explained

Diversification means spreading your money across many investments so that no single one can do too much damage. The idea is captured by the saying "don't put all your eggs in one basket."

## Why it works

Different investments do not all rise and fall at the same time. When one company has bad news, others may be unaffected. When stocks fall, high-quality bonds have often held steadier. By combining investments that do not move in lockstep, a portfolio's overall ups and downs can be smaller than those of its individual parts, without necessarily giving up much long-term return.

## Levels of diversification

You can diversify within an asset class, for example owning hundreds of companies instead of five. You can diversify across sectors, so a downturn in technology or energy does not dominate your results. You can diversify across asset classes, such as stocks, bonds and cash, and across geographies, such as US and international markets.

## Concentration risk

Concentration risk is the opposite of diversification: a large share of your money depends on one company, one sector or one country. A common example is holding a lot of your employer's stock, which ties both your paycheck and your savings to the same company. A portfolio of five technology stocks may feel diversified because it has several names, but they often move together.

## Measuring it simply

One simple measure looks at how much of a portfolio sits in its largest holdings and sectors. If the top position is more than about a fifth of the total, or one sector is more than about two fifths, the portfolio is generally considered concentrated. Broad index funds give instant diversification because each share holds hundreds or thousands of securities.

## What diversification cannot do

Diversification reduces the risk tied to individual companies, but it cannot remove market risk, the chance that most investments fall together, as they did in 2008 and early 2020. It does not guarantee a profit or protect against loss.
