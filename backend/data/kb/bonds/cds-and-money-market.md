# Certificates of Deposit (CDs)

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a savings product from a bank or credit union that pays a fixed interest rate for a set term, such as six months, one year or five years. In return for the higher rate, you agree to leave the money untouched until the term ends.

## How CDs work

You deposit a lump sum, the rate is locked in, and at maturity you get your money back plus interest. Longer terms usually pay higher rates, though not always. CDs at FDIC-insured banks and NCUA-insured credit unions are insured up to the standard limits.

## Early withdrawal penalties

Taking money out before maturity usually costs a penalty, often several months of interest. Some "no-penalty" CDs allow early withdrawal at a slightly lower rate.

## Brokered CDs

Brokerage firms also sell CDs issued by banks. These brokered CDs can be sold on a secondary market before maturity instead of paying a penalty, but their market price can fall if interest rates rise, so you may get back less than you put in if you sell early. Some are callable by the issuing bank.

## CD ladders

A CD ladder spreads money across CDs with staggered maturities, for example one, two, three, four and five years. As each CD matures, it can be spent or reinvested at the longest term. Laddering balances access to cash with higher longer-term rates.

## Where CDs fit

CDs suit money with a known time frame that must not lose value, such as a down payment due in two years. The main risks are inflation outpacing the fixed rate and missing out if rates rise after you lock in.
