# Corporate Bonds and Credit Ratings

Companies issue corporate bonds to borrow money from investors. Corporate bonds usually pay higher interest than Treasuries because they carry credit risk: the chance the company cannot make its payments.

## Credit ratings

Rating agencies such as S&P Global, Moody's and Fitch grade bond issuers' creditworthiness.

- **Investment grade**: BBB- (S&P) or Baa3 (Moody's) and above. Lower expected default risk.
- **High yield** (also called junk bonds): below those levels. Higher interest to compensate for a meaningfully higher risk of default.

Ratings are opinions, not guarantees, and can change. A downgrade typically pushes a bond's price down.

## Credit spreads

The extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury of similar maturity is its credit spread. Spreads tend to widen when investors worry about the economy, which pushes corporate bond prices down, and narrow when conditions improve.

## How high-yield bonds behave

High-yield bonds often move more like stocks than like Treasuries. In recessions, defaults rise and their prices can fall sharply, just when investors most want stability. This is why many portfolios use high-quality bonds, rather than high-yield ones, as a stabilizing ballast.

## Other features to know

- **Callable bonds** can be repaid early by the issuer, usually when rates fall, which limits your upside.
- **Seniority** determines who is paid first in a bankruptcy; secured and senior bonds rank above subordinated ones.

Corporate bond funds spread credit risk across hundreds of issuers, which most individuals cannot easily do with individual bonds.
