# Bond Duration Explained

Duration measures how sensitive a bond or bond fund's price is to changes in interest rates. It is expressed in years and is one of the most useful numbers on a bond fund's fact sheet.

## The rule of thumb

For a small change in interest rates, a bond's price changes by roughly its duration times the rate change, in the opposite direction. A fund with a duration of 6 years would be expected to lose about 6% if rates rose by 1 percentage point, and gain about 6% if they fell by 1 point.

## What affects duration

- **Maturity**: longer maturities mean longer duration.
- **Coupon**: higher coupons shorten duration, because more of the bond's value is paid back earlier.
- **Yield level**: higher yields slightly shorten duration.
- **Call features**: callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities can have durations that change as rates move.

A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity, since all its value arrives at the end.

## Matching duration to your horizon

A useful idea for bond investors: if your time horizon is roughly equal to a bond fund's duration, rising rates hurt the price in the short run but the fund reinvests at higher yields, and the two effects tend to offset over that horizon. Short-term needs are generally matched with short-duration bonds.

## Limits of the measure

Duration is a linear approximation. For large rate changes, convexity (the curvature of the price-yield relationship) makes the actual result differ. Duration also says nothing about credit risk: a short-duration junk bond fund can still fall sharply in a recession.
