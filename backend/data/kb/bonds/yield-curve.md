# The Yield Curve

The yield curve is a chart of interest rates on bonds of the same credit quality, usually US Treasuries, across different maturities, from short-term bills to 30-year bonds.

## Normal, flat and inverted

- **Normal (upward sloping)**: long-term yields are higher than short-term yields. Investors usually want extra compensation, a term premium, for locking money up longer and bearing more interest rate risk.
- **Flat**: short and long yields are similar.
- **Inverted**: short-term yields are higher than long-term yields.

## What shapes the curve

Short-term yields are heavily influenced by the Federal Reserve's policy rate. Long-term yields reflect expectations for future short-term rates, expected inflation and the term premium. If investors expect the Fed to cut rates in the future, long-term yields can fall below short-term ones.

## Inversions and recessions

An inverted yield curve, especially between 3-month bills or 2-year notes and 10-year notes, has preceded most US recessions in recent decades. The reasoning is that markets expect weaker growth and lower future rates. But the timing has varied widely, and inversions are not a precise forecasting tool.

## Why it matters to everyday savers

- When the curve is inverted, short-term savings and T-bills may pay as much as or more than long-term bonds.
- Banks borrow short and lend long, so the curve's shape affects lending and mortgage rates.
- The curve's shape helps explain why long-term bond funds and short-term funds can behave very differently in the same year.
