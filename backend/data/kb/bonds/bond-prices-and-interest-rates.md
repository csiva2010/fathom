# Why Bond Prices Fall When Interest Rates Rise

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. This seesaw relationship explains why bond funds can lose value even though bonds are considered safer than stocks.

## The basic logic

Imagine you own a bond paying 3% interest. If new bonds of similar quality start paying 5%, no one will pay full price for your 3% bond. Its market price must fall until its effective yield is competitive with the new bonds. When rates fall, the reverse happens: older bonds with higher coupons become more valuable, and their prices rise.

## Yield to maturity

A bond's yield to maturity is the total annual return you would earn if you bought it at today's price and held it until it matures, with every payment made on time. When a bond's price falls, its yield to maturity rises, and vice versa.

## Holding to maturity

If you own an individual bond and hold it to maturity, and the issuer does not default, you receive the full face value back regardless of price swings along the way. Price changes matter if you sell early. Bond funds never mature, so their prices reflect rate changes continuously, though the fund gradually reinvests at the new, higher yields.

## Which bonds are most sensitive

- **Longer maturities** are more sensitive, because their fixed payments stretch further into the future.
- **Lower coupons** are more sensitive than high coupons of the same maturity.
- **Duration** summarizes this sensitivity in one number: a duration of 6 means roughly a 6% price drop for a 1 percentage point rise in rates.

## The upside of rising rates

Rising rates hurt existing bond prices in the short run, but they mean new money and reinvested interest earn more going forward. Long-term bond investors can end up better off after rates rise.
