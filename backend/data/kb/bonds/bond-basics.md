# Bond Basics

A bond is a loan you make to a government or company. In return, the borrower promises to pay you interest, called the coupon, on a set schedule and to repay the original amount, called the principal or face value, on a set date known as maturity.

## Who issues bonds

The US Treasury issues Treasury bills, notes and bonds, which are backed by the federal government. States and cities issue municipal bonds, whose interest is often exempt from federal income tax. Companies issue corporate bonds, which usually pay more interest than Treasuries because the risk of the company failing to pay is higher.

## Why bond prices change

Bonds can be bought and sold before they mature, and their prices move. The main driver is interest rates. When market interest rates rise, existing bonds that pay lower rates become less attractive, so their prices fall. When rates fall, existing bonds become more valuable. Longer-maturity bonds are more sensitive to rate changes; this sensitivity is measured by duration.

## Yield

Yield is the return you earn on a bond relative to its price. If you buy a bond below its face value, your yield is higher than its coupon rate; if you pay above face value, it is lower. Yield to maturity estimates your total annual return if you hold the bond until it matures and every payment is made.

## Risks

Credit risk is the chance the issuer cannot pay. Rating agencies grade issuers; lower-rated bonds, often called high-yield or junk bonds, pay more to compensate for that risk. Interest-rate risk is the price drop when rates rise. Inflation risk is the loss of buying power when fixed payments do not keep pace with prices.

## Role in a portfolio

Bonds have historically been less volatile than stocks and often provide steady income, which is why many diversified portfolios hold some bonds, especially as an investor approaches the date they need the money.
