# Bond Funds vs Individual Bonds

Investors can own bonds directly or through mutual funds and ETFs. Both approaches have trade-offs.

## Individual bonds

When you buy an individual bond and hold it to maturity, you know exactly what you will receive, assuming no default: the scheduled interest payments and the face value at the end. Price swings in between do not matter if you do not sell. This predictability suits a known future expense, and building a "ladder" of bonds maturing in different years is a common strategy.

Drawbacks: diversifying across many issuers takes a lot of money, trading costs and bid-ask spreads can be high for small orders (except Treasuries), and you must manage reinvestment yourself.

## Bond funds

A bond fund holds hundreds or thousands of bonds, giving instant diversification, professional management, easy reinvestment of interest, and the ability to buy and sell any day. Expense ratios on broad bond index funds can be very low.

The main difference: a bond fund has no maturity date. It continually replaces maturing bonds, so its duration stays roughly constant and its price fluctuates with rates indefinitely. There is no fixed date on which you are guaranteed to get your principal back.

## Which fits when?

- For credit-risky bonds such as corporates or munis, funds usually provide far better diversification.
- For Treasuries and CDs, individual holdings are easy to buy and work well for a dated goal.
- For long-term, ongoing investing, a low-cost bond fund is simple and widely used.

In practice, the long-run results of a bond fund and a rolling ladder with similar duration tend to be similar.
