# Safe Withdrawal Rates and the 4% Rule

The 4% rule is a widely cited guideline for how much someone can withdraw from a retirement portfolio each year without running out of money over about 30 years.

## The original idea

Research in the 1990s, including the Trinity study, tested historical US market returns. It found that withdrawing 4% of a stock and bond portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjusting that dollar amount for inflation each year, would have lasted at least 30 years in nearly all historical periods. With a $1 million portfolio, that means $40,000 in year one, rising with inflation.

## What it assumes

- A roughly 30-year retirement.
- A balanced portfolio, often 50% to 75% stocks.
- Withdrawals that rise with inflation regardless of how markets perform.
- US historical returns, which were strong by global standards.

## Common criticisms and adjustments

- Longer retirements, such as for early retirees, may call for lower rates.
- Expected future returns may differ from the past.
- Real retirees adjust spending; flexible strategies, such as cutting withdrawals after bad years, can support higher average withdrawals.
- Fees reduce the sustainable rate.

## A rule of thumb, not a plan

The 4% rule is useful for rough estimates, for example, multiplying annual spending needs by 25 gives a ballpark savings target. It is not a guarantee. Social Security, pensions, annuities, taxes and personal flexibility all shape a real withdrawal plan.
