# How a 401(k) Works

A 401(k) is a retirement savings plan offered by many US employers. You choose a percentage of each paycheck to contribute, and the money is invested in funds offered by the plan. The account gets special tax treatment to encourage long-term saving.

## Traditional and Roth contributions

With traditional contributions, money goes in before income tax, which lowers your taxable income today. Investments grow tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Many plans also offer Roth 401(k) contributions, which are made after tax; qualified withdrawals in retirement, including the growth, are tax-free.

## The employer match

Many employers match part of what you contribute, for example 50 cents for every dollar up to 6% of your salary. The match is effectively part of your pay. Educators commonly point out that contributing at least enough to get the full match is one of the highest-return steps available to a saver, because the match is an immediate return on your contribution.

## Vesting

Your own contributions are always yours. Employer contributions may vest over time, meaning you earn full ownership of them only after working for the employer for a certain number of years. If you leave earlier, you may forfeit the unvested part.

## Limits and rules

The IRS sets an annual limit on how much employees can contribute, with an extra catch-up amount allowed from age 50. Limits change most years, so check the IRS website for the current figures. Withdrawals before age 59½ are generally subject to income tax plus a 10% additional tax, with some exceptions.

## Investment choices

Plans usually offer a menu of funds, often including target-date funds that automatically shift from stocks toward bonds as the target retirement year approaches. Paying attention to each fund's expense ratio is worthwhile because fees compound over decades.

## When you change jobs

You can usually leave the money in the old plan, roll it into a new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Cashing out triggers taxes and often penalties.
