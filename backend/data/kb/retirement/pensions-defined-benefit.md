# Pensions: Defined Benefit Plans

A pension, formally a defined benefit plan, promises a specific monthly income in retirement, usually based on your salary and years of service. It is less common in private companies today, but many government workers, teachers and union members still have one.

## Defined benefit versus defined contribution

- **Defined benefit (pension)**: the employer promises the benefit and bears the investment risk. A typical formula might be 1.5% times years of service times final average salary.
- **Defined contribution (401(k), 403(b))**: you and your employer contribute, and your retirement income depends on how much was saved and how the investments performed. You bear the investment risk.

## Key features

- **Vesting**: you must work a certain number of years to earn a right to the benefit.
- **Payout options**: single life (highest monthly amount, ends at your death) or joint and survivor (lower monthly amount, continues for a spouse).
- **Lump sum offers**: some plans let you take a one-time payment instead of monthly income. Comparing the two depends on health, interest rates, other income and comfort managing investments.
- **Inflation**: many private pensions do not increase with inflation; many public ones do, at least partly.

## Protection

Most private-sector pensions are insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) up to legal limits if the plan fails. Public pensions are backed by their state or local government rather than the PBGC.

## Fitting a pension into a plan

A pension works like a bond-like income stream. People who will receive one sometimes consider it when deciding how much risk to take with their other savings.
