# Retirement Account Rollovers

A rollover moves money from one retirement account to another, most often from a former employer's 401(k) into an IRA or a new employer's plan, without paying taxes or penalties if done correctly.

## Options when you leave a job

- Leave the money in the old plan, if allowed.
- Roll it into your new employer's plan, if it accepts rollovers.
- Roll it into an IRA.
- Cash it out, which usually triggers income tax and, before age 59½, often a 10% additional tax.

## Direct versus indirect rollovers

- **Direct rollover**: the old plan sends the money straight to the new account. No taxes are withheld. This is the simplest and safest method.
- **Indirect (60-day) rollover**: you receive a check and must deposit the full amount into a retirement account within 60 days. Plans must withhold 20% for taxes, so to roll over the full amount you need to make up that 20% from other funds; any amount not rolled over is taxable. You can do only one IRA-to-IRA indirect rollover in any 12-month period.

## Matching account types

Traditional pre-tax money rolls into a traditional IRA or plan. Roth 401(k) money rolls into a Roth IRA or Roth plan account. Rolling pre-tax money into a Roth is a conversion and is taxable.

## Things to compare

People typically compare investment choices, fees, creditor protections (which can differ between plans and IRAs), the ability to borrow from a plan, and the effect on future backdoor Roth contributions before deciding.
